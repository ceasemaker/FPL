\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Fantasy Premier League (FPL) managers choose, every week, eleven starters from a fifteen-player
squad and a captain whose points are doubled. We forecast each player's points for the coming
gameweek with gradient-boosted regression trees and evaluate them on four seasons of data
(2022/23--2025/26), with expanding training windows that contain only earlier weeks. Over 114
test gameweeks (2023/24--2025/26) and every registered player, the forecast is more accurate than
recent form, a fixture-adjusted form rule and a ridge regression (forecasts exist for every player
with at least one earlier club-fixture week). It also ranks realistic
selection candidates better within each week. Its decisions are less clearly better. As a captain
rule it outscores the most expensive attacker who played at least 60 minutes per fixture in his
previous club-fixture week by \vsPremiumMean{}
points per week (95\% bootstrap interval \vsPremiumLow{} to \vsPremiumHigh{}), almost entirely
because of one season, and unadjusted recent form by \vsFormMean{}. Its advantages over a form
rule scaled by the coming week's fixture count and over a ridge regression are not distinguishable
from zero. It trails the captain most often chosen by the managers who finished each season in the
overall top 100 by \vsEliteMean{} (\vsEliteLow{} to \vsEliteHigh{}); that cohort is defined after
the season, so this is a retrospective reference. The model does not meet a decision criterion
recorded before the first evaluation. A football adaptation of basketball's usage rate reduced
forecast error by about 0.3\% and had no detectable effect on decisions. Performance varies
markedly between seasons, and resolving captaincy differences of a point per week or less needs
several seasons of prospective evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Fantasy Premier League is the official fantasy game of the English Premier League. Each manager
holds fifteen players within a fixed budget and, before each of the season's 38 gameweek
deadlines, names eleven starters and a captain whose points are doubled \citep{fplrules}. Players
score for playing time, goals, assists, clean sheets, saves and bonus points and, from 2025/26,
for defensive actions. Two decisions recur every week regardless of transfers: who starts, and
who captains. Both reduce to ranking players by expected points in the coming week.

A small literature forecasts FPL points. Early work treated team selection as sequential
decision-making under uncertainty \citep{matthews2012}. Later studies forecast points from recent
match histories using time-series and recurrent models \citep{gupta2019}, convolutional and
recurrent networks over a player's last five matches \citep{lombu2024}, and ridge regression,
gradient boosting and convolutional networks \citep{frees2024}. Others add news and betting-market
information \citep{bonello2019} or couple forecasts with mixed-integer squad selection
\citep{ramezani2025}. These studies mostly report forecast error or season-long team totals. We
ask whether a forecast makes better \emph{weekly decisions} than simple rules and expert consensus,
judged against a criterion fixed before the results were seen.

Two issues receive particular attention: information leakage, meaning the use of anything not
known at decision time \citep{tashman2000}, and the noise of weekly outcomes, which limits what any
evaluation can show. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a deadline-ordered feature set that carries players' form across seasons;
  \item a walk-forward evaluation that retrains every week on every registered player, and
        compares decisions with mechanical, statistical and expert comparators;
  \item lineup evaluation on 11{,}400 real squads, split into the eleven and the armband;
  \item ablation, sensitivity and dependence analyses; and
  \item a test of whether ball usage, adapted from basketball \citep{bbrefglossary,oliver2004},
        adds information.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data}

\subsection{Player-gameweek records}
We use official FPL data for four complete seasons, 2022/23 to 2025/26 \citep{fplapi}. These
weekly records were collected by the author during each season. Fixture lists and club identifiers
for past seasons come from a public archive of the same source \citep{anandfpl}. For every
registered player and gameweek we record:
\begin{itemize}
  \item minutes, starts, goals, assists, clean sheets, goals conceded and saves;
  \item bonus points and the bonus points system score;
  \item expected goals (xG), expected assists (xA) and expected goals conceded;
  \item total FPL points.
\end{itemize}
Expected goals estimate the probability that a shot is scored given its characteristics
\citep{rathke2017}; FPL publishes Opta's values. Double gameweeks, in which a club plays twice, are
summed. Players and clubs keep the same identifier across seasons.

Every registered player is kept, including those who never played that season
(Table~\ref{tab:data}). Whether a player will play is not known at earlier deadlines, so removing
those who end the season without minutes would select the evaluated population with hindsight.

Weekly prices were not archived, so each player carries his start-of-season price. The 2025/26
scoring rules added points for defensive actions \citep{fplrules}, which partly explains that
season's higher total; a season indicator lets the model treat it separately.

\paragraph{Fixtures.} Fixture assignments are the final ones. When a match is postponed, its
original gameweek becomes a blank for both clubs and its new gameweek may become a double. At the
original deadline the match was still expected, so final assignments contain some hindsight about
which clubs play. We cannot reconstruct the schedule as it stood at each deadline. Instead, we
report results separately for the \nRegularWeeks{} test weeks in which every club played exactly
once and the \nIrregularWeeks{} weeks with a blank or double gameweek
(Table~\ref{tab:captainrobust}). We also assume that a gameweek's outcomes were known by the next
deadline, which holds for completed gameweeks in FPL's schedule.

\subsection{Squads of top-ranked managers}
For 2023/24 to 2025/26 we hold the weekly fifteen-player squads, starting elevens, bench order,
captain and vice-captain of the 100 managers who finished each season in the overall top 100:
$3\times38\times100=11{,}400$ squad-weeks. Membership of this cohort is determined by the end of
the season, so the cohort is selected on the outcome \citep{brown1992}. We use its most-captained
player as a retrospective reference and its squads as a realistic test bed. Lineup results are
therefore conditional on these managers' squads.

\subsection{Season event data}
For the usage analysis (Section~\ref{sec:usage}) we use Opta season totals for every Premier
League player from 2021/22 to 2025/26, published through the Premier League's statistics services
\citep{plstats}. Each player is assigned to the club whose season squad lists him or, for players
who left mid-season, to the club where he played most of his minutes.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Notation and information ordering}
Index players by $i$, seasons by $s$ and gameweeks by $g$, and write $t=(s,g)$ for a point in time
ordered lexicographically. Let $y_{it}$ be player $i$'s points in week $t$, $m_{it}$ his minutes and
$n_{it}\in\{0,1,2\}$ the number of fixtures his club plays. Features for week $t$ are computed from
weeks $t'<t$ and from quantities fixed before the deadline: position, start-of-season price,
previous-season totals and the fixture list (subject to the caveat above). We check the ordering by
perturbing the outcomes of one week and confirming that features for that week and all earlier
weeks are unchanged.

\subsection{Features}
A player's state is updated after each gameweek in which his club had a fixture (a
\emph{club-fixture week}); the features for week $t$ take the latest state from a strictly earlier
week. Table~\ref{tab:choices} lists the design choices with their basis.

\paragraph{Form and role.} Minutes per fixture in the last club-fixture week and averaged over the
last three and six; the average, over the last six, of starts per fixture; and points per fixture
averaged over the last three and six. Rolling windows of recent matches are the common input in
FPL forecasting \citep{lombu2024,frees2024,ramezani2025}. Windows run across season boundaries.

\paragraph{Per-90 rates.} Over the last six club-fixture weeks $\mathcal{W}_6$, for a statistic
$z$ (xG, xA, bonus, bonus points system score, saves),
\[
  r^{(6)}_{it}(z) = 90 \cdot \frac{\sum_{t'\in\mathcal{W}_6} z_{it'}}{\max\!\bigl(\sum_{t'\in\mathcal{W}_6} m_{it'},\,90\bigr)},
\]
where the floor of 90 minutes stops a short cameo from producing an extreme rate.

\paragraph{Season to date.} The same rates for xG, xA and points accumulated over the current
season, the share of available minutes played, $\sum m / (90\sum n)$, and the number of fixtures
so far. In a season's first week the rates are missing and the fixture count is zero.

\paragraph{Prior season.} The player's xG, xA and points per 90, and his share of the season's
minutes, in the previous season. The motivation is by analogy with empirical Bayes estimation,
which improves noisy early-season rates by combining them with a longer-run prior
\citep{efronmorris1975,brown2008}. The model is not an empirical Bayes estimator; the trees decide
how to use these inputs, and Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} tests whether they help.

\paragraph{Club and opponent strength.} For club $c$ in week $t$, let
$G^{+}_{ct}=\sum_{i\in c}\mathrm{xG}_{it}$. As a proxy for xG conceded we take
$G^{-}_{ct}=\sum_{o} G^{+}_{ot}/n_{ot}$ over its opponents $o$ that week. In a double gameweek this
splits an opponent's weekly xG equally across its fixtures, an approximation forced by weekly
rather than match-level data. Over the club's previous eight club-fixture weeks, which can span
two seasons,
\[
  A_{ct} = \frac{\sum_{t'} G^{+}_{ct'}}{\sum_{t'} n_{ct'}}, \qquad
  D_{ct} = \frac{\sum_{t'} G^{-}_{ct'}}{\sum_{t'} n_{ct'}}.
\]
Separate attacking and defensive strengths per club are the basis of the standard goal models for
football \citep{maher1982,dixoncoles1997}. We build them from xG because goals are rare and xG
summarises the quality of the chances a team creates and concedes \citep{rathke2017}. The player
receives his club's $A$ and $D$, his opponents' average $A$ and $D$ in week $t$, his number of
fixtures and the share played at home.

\paragraph{Static and exposure.} Position, start-of-season price, gameweek number, an indicator
for the defensive-action scoring rules, the number of earlier weeks in which he played, and the
number of gameweeks since his last club-fixture week.

\subsection{Model}
The target is $y_{it}$. We fit histogram-based gradient-boosted regression trees
\citep{friedman2001}, in scikit-learn's implementation \citep{pedregosa2011}, which follows the
histogram approach of \citet{ke2017}; no LightGBM model was fitted. The loss is squared error.
The settings are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item learning rate 0.05 and at most 250 boosting iterations, with the library's default early
        stopping above 10{,}000 training rows (training stops when the loss on a random 10\% of
        the training window fails to improve for ten iterations);
  \item at most 31 leaves per tree and at least 80 observations per leaf;
  \item an $L_2$ penalty of 1.0.
\end{itemize}
They start from the library defaults (learning rate 0.1, 100 iterations, 20 observations per leaf,
no penalty) and move each setting in the conservative direction suited to a noisy target. In the
114 weekly fits, early stopping ended training after \iterMin{} to \iterMax{} iterations (median
\iterMedian). The internal validation split uses only the training window, so it does not leak
information from the test week. Missing inputs are handled natively by the trees. Features that are
constant or entirely missing within a training window are dropped for that fit. A player with no
earlier club-fixture week receives no forecast. Training uses every player-week with a fixture and
at least one earlier club-fixture week.

\subsection{Decision rules}
\paragraph{Captain.} The model captains the player with the highest forecast among those whose
club has a fixture. The captain endpoint is the nominated player's own points that week, single
and without vice-captain fallback.

\paragraph{Lineup.} Given a fifteen-player squad, the model chooses the legal formation that
maximises the sum of forecasts. A formation has one goalkeeper, $d$ defenders, $m$ midfielders and
$f$ forwards, with $d\in\{3,4,5\}$, $m\in\{2,\dots,5\}$, $f\in\{1,2,3\}$ and $d+m+f=10$. The two
highest forecasts in the eleven are captain and vice-captain. The bench lists the reserve
goalkeeper first and the outfield players by forecast. Lineups are scored by the game's ordinary
rules \citep{fplrules}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a starter with no minutes is replaced by the first bench player, in bench order, who played
        and whose entry keeps the formation legal;
  \item the captain's points are doubled, or the vice-captain's if the captain did not play;
  \item chips are ignored (a triple captain counts as an ordinary captain, a bench boost as an
        ordinary week).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Comparators}
\begin{description}
  \item[Premium attacker.] The midfielder or forward with the highest start-of-season price among
        those who played at least 60 minutes per fixture in their previous club-fixture week. Exact
        price ties are broken by points per fixture over the last six, then by player identifier.
  \item[Recent form.] The highest points per fixture over the last six, among players averaging at
        least 60 minutes per fixture over the last three.
  \item[Top-100 consensus.] The player most often captained that week by the managers who finished
        the season in the overall top 100 (ties to the lower identifier). Not available at the
        deadline; a retrospective reference.
\end{description}
Two further comparators were added after an independent review, so they are not part of the
decision criterion.
\emph{Recent form $\times$ fixtures} multiplies recent form by the number of fixtures in the coming
week. \emph{Ridge regression} is a walk-forward linear model on start-of-season price, recent
minutes, position and the number of fixtures. For lineups, the comparators are the managers' own
eleven, bench order and captain, and the model's selection procedure applied to each comparator's
ranking.

\subsection{Evaluation}
For each test week $w$ the model is retrained on all weeks before $w$, including earlier weeks of
the same season, and used to forecast week $w$. This rolling-origin design mirrors live use
\citep{tashman2000}. Random cross-validation can misstate accuracy when observations are
dependent over time \citep{bergmeir2012}; here, rolling form and the season structure create
exactly that dependence. The test period is the 114 gameweeks of 2023/24, 2024/25 and 2025/26.

Comparisons are paired by week, so rules are judged on the same outcomes
\citep{dieboldmariano1995}. For rule $A$ and comparator $B$, let
$d_w=\mathrm{pts}_w(A)-\mathrm{pts}_w(B)$; for lineups, $\mathrm{pts}_w$ is first averaged over the 100
squads in week $w$. The primary interval resamples the 114 weekly differences with replacement
\citep{efron1993}, 10{,}000 times. Because consecutive weeks may be dependent, we also report a
moving-block bootstrap with blocks of six weeks that never cross a season
\citep{kunsch1989,politis1994}, the lag-one autocorrelation of $d_w$, and season-by-season
estimates. Inference refers to these three seasons; extrapolating to future seasons assumes the
game and the data stay comparable.

\paragraph{Decision criterion.} Written into the evaluation code before the first run, and
recorded in that code's first version-controlled commit: the model's captain choice meets the
criterion only if the lower end of the interval is above zero against \emph{every} comparator in
the original set (premium attacker, recent form, top-100 consensus). This is an
intersection--union rule, so no multiplicity adjustment is needed. The same rule applies to
lineups. The study was not formally registered, so this pre-specification is reported by the
author.

\paragraph{Forecast accuracy.} We report the mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error and
Pearson correlation over all test player-weeks with a fixture. We also report the mean, over
weeks, of the within-week Spearman rank correlation, and the same measures among \emph{regulars}
(players averaging at least 60 minutes per fixture over the last three), who are the realistic
selection candidates.

\section{Extension: ball usage}\label{sec:usage}

\subsection{Motivation and definition}
In basketball, usage percentage estimates the share of a team's possessions a player ends with a
shot, free throws or a turnover while on the floor \citep{bbrefglossary}, building on the
possession framework of \citet{oliver2004}. A football adaptation
\citep{sincerefc2025a,sincerefc2025b} counts \emph{possession-ending actions}: shots, and every way
of losing the ball. It argues that the measure describes a player's role within a team, and that
a player used to being the main option may struggle at a club that already has one. If so,
last season's usage could inform this season's output, particularly after a transfer.

We count a player's actions as Opta's possessions lost plus his shots. A shot does not always end
a possession, so this is an approximation. Possessions lost covers incomplete passes and crosses,
failed take-ons, being tackled and miscontrols, and more: for Premier League players with at least
1{,}500 minutes in 2023/24--2025/26, it is a median \possMedianLow--\possMedianHigh\% larger than
the sum of those itemised components, depending on the season (the seasons' 5th and 95th
percentiles span \possPLow--\possPHigh\% larger). We
then compute a minutes-adjusted share of the team's recorded actions, following the basketball
formula with eleven players in place of five:
\[
  \mathrm{USG}_i = 100\cdot\frac{\mathrm{PEA}_i\cdot(\text{team minutes}/11)}{\text{minutes}_i\cdot\mathrm{PEA}_{\text{team}}}.
\]
Team totals sum every player of that club with recorded actions. Season totals cannot isolate the
team's actions while the player was on the pitch. A player who changed clubs mid-season has all his
season's actions assigned to one club. For creation we use Opta's chances created (passes leading
directly to a shot); the adaptation's broader shot-creating actions are not available to us.

\subsection{Features and test}
Six features summarise the previous season:
\begin{itemize}
  \item usage rate;
  \item possession-ending actions per 90;
  \item chances created per 90 and per action;
  \item touches in the opposition box per 90;
  \item club possession share.

\subsection{Result}
Coverage is measured on the usage rate itself, over all 84{,}995 test player-weeks with a fixture
(including the 795 without earlier history, which receive no forecast). The usage rate is present
for \usageCover{} of these player-weeks and \usageCoverRegulars{} of regulars' player-weeks. Of all test player-weeks, \usageMissNoPL{} belong to players with no
Premier League minutes the season before. A further \usageMissOther{} belong to players who had
minutes but no matched or complete usage record. Players who had changed club account for
\usageMovers{} of test player-weeks.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Accuracy.} Weekly MAE falls by \usageMaeGain{}: detectable, but about 0.3\% of the
        error. For players who changed club the change is \usageMoverGain{}.
  \item \emph{Decisions.} Captain points change by \usageCapGain{} and lineup points by
        \usageLineGain{}, neither distinguishable from zero.
\end{itemize}
In this implementation and sample, last season's usage adds a small amount of information about
points and no detectable value for decisions. The intervals do not exclude captain gains of up to
\usageCapHigh{} points per week. A match-level, in-season measure, or a club-level model of how a
signing redistributes the ball, may behave differently.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.} The forecast is more accurate than every forecasting
comparator, ranks regulars better within each week, and passes a pooled linear calibration
check. Better forecasts do
not yet translate into clearly better decisions. As a captain rule the model beats a pure price
rule and unadjusted form, but not by a detectable margin once the comparator accounts for double
gameweeks or is itself a simple regression. In lineups it beats both form rules, including the
fixture-scaled one, and shows no detectable difference from the regression. The undetected
captain differences are under a point per week, which three seasons resolve poorly (see the
precision and power analysis below).

\paragraph{The top-100 comparison.} The model trails the top-100 consensus. The data cannot say
how much of that gap is information the model lacks, how much is skill, and how much is selection.
The cohort is chosen after the season, so its choices are, on average, ones that worked out
\citep{brown1992}. At the deadline managers also knew team news, late injuries and penalty duty;
earlier work found that adding news and betting-market information improves FPL forecasts
\citep{bonello2019}. A cohort fixed before the season, such as the previous season's top managers,
would be the fair deployable benchmark; we did not have its weekly squads.

\paragraph{Precision and power.} The standard deviation of the weekly captain difference ranges
from \sdMin{} to \sdMax{} points across comparators. Taking $\sigma=5$ for illustration and assuming
independent weeks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A 95\% interval of half-width $h$ needs about $(1.96\,\sigma/h)^2$ weeks: 96 weeks for $h=1$,
        384 for $h=0.5$. One season gives $h\approx1.6$.
  \item Detecting a true difference $\delta$ with 80\% power in a two-sided 5\% test needs about
        $\bigl((1.96+0.84)\,\sigma/\delta\bigr)^2$ weeks: 196 weeks (about five seasons) for
        $\delta=1$ and 784 (about twenty) for $\delta=0.5$.
\end{itemize}
A season of results therefore says little about captaincy rules that differ by a point a week or
less, and larger $\sigma$ makes this worse. Differences between variants of the same model need their own paired estimates, which
Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} does not provide.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Information.} No team news, injury history, set-piece duties or bookmaker prices.
  \item \emph{Schedules.} Final fixture assignments contain some hindsight about postponements;
        regular-week results are reported for comparison.
  \item \emph{Prices.} Only start-of-season prices are available historically.
  \item \emph{Outcome-selected cohort.} The top-100 comparisons are retrospective.
  \item \emph{Proxies.} Weekly rather than match-level data force the xG-conceded allocation and
        season-total usage measures described above.
  \item \emph{Reuse.} The usage extension and the added comparators were evaluated on the same
        seasons as the main analysis; confirmation should be prospective.
  \item \emph{Usage measurement.} We did not check the completeness of team denominators or the
        sensitivity of usage to minimum-minute thresholds.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Prospective protocol.} From gameweek 7 of 2026/27, three captains are logged before each
deadline and frozen at it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the model's;
  \item the one with the highest official expected-points figure;
  \item a live premium rule.
\end{itemize}
The live premium rule differs from the historical one. It uses the current price, a start in the
previous calendar gameweek and current form, because that information is available live. The
most-captained player across all managers is recorded once the gameweek begins. Official expected
points, ownership and availability flags are archived at each deadline.

\section{Conclusion}
Evaluated week by week over three seasons on every registered player, a gradient-boosted forecast
predicts and ranks players better than form-based and regression comparators. Its captain choices
beat naive rules but are not distinguishable from those of a fixture-aware form rule or a simple
regression. Its lineups beat both form rules and are not distinguishable from the regression's.
Both fall short of the choices of each season's eventual top 100 managers.
Distinguishing rules that differ by a point a week or less needs several seasons of evidence, so
further evaluation should be prospective and specified in advance.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} All results were produced in one software environment (Python 3.11,
scikit-learn 1.9.1, pandas 3.0.6, NumPy 2.4.6) from the analysis scripts accompanying this paper.
The numbers in the results sections, tables and figures are generated from the result files by
script; Table~\ref{tab:data} and the illustrative precision and power calculations are written by
hand. Weekly captain selections and outcomes are archived with the scripts; player-level forecasts
and weekly lineup differences are not.
